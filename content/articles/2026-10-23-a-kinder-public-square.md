We do not inherit a kinder public square. We build it, practice by practice, in places ordinary and unglamorous: a school board meeting, a group chat, a line at the polls, the comments beneath a story online.

It is tempting to treat civility as a mood — something that descends on a society in good times and lifts in bad ones. But moods cannot be chosen. Practices can. A practice is something a person does on purpose, repeatedly, especially when it is inconvenient. Civility understood this way is less about tone than about a handful of habits.

The first is listening long enough to be able to state the other side's view in terms its holders would accept. This is harder than it sounds, and rarer. Most public argument is conducted against a caricature, which is why so much of it changes no one's mind. A person who can describe an opponent's position fairly has earned the right to disagree with it.

The second is separating what we know from what we fear. Elections concentrate both. In the weeks before a vote, rumors move faster than facts, and the most alarming claim is often the most widely shared. The habit of asking "How do we know that?" — and waiting for the answer — is not timidity. It is a form of respect, for the truth and for the people who would be harmed by a falsehood.

The third is keeping faith with the rules even when they do not favor us. A democracy depends on losers accepting outcomes they dislike, through processes they can verify, with the confidence that they may win next time. That confidence is not automatic. It is sustained by institutions that count fairly and by citizens who insist that they do, whichever side benefits.

None of this requires agreement. A kinder public square is not a quieter one; it is one in which disagreement can be loud without becoming contempt. The goal is not to end the argument but to keep it going — to make it possible for people who see the world differently to keep talking, keep voting and keep living as neighbors.

That is a practice, not a sentiment. It begins with how we speak, what we attend to, and the trust we keep with people we are unlikely to agree with. It begins, in other words, today.
